Threat brief

Your assessment says what is exposed and what is weak. The threat brief says who would care, what they would try first, and which fixes are worth doing before the others.

A brief is built from your own findings, not a generic industry report. It follows an exposure and vulnerability assessment.

What the brief answers

What applies to you

Most published attack methods are irrelevant to any one business. The brief works out which ones fit your exposed systems, your sector and your size, and sets the rest aside so you are not paying attention to noise.

What would be tried first

Working from your own findings, the brief explains the most likely routes in and what they would reach: which accounts, which records, which systems, and what that would cost you in practice.

Where to spend first

Recommendations ranked by how much risk each one removes for the effort involved, so a limited security budget goes to the fixes that matter instead of the longest list.

What a threat brief includes

A short written brief in plain language, aimed at whoever decides what to spend, with the supporting technical detail kept separate for your IT provider. Each conclusion names the assessment finding it rests on and how confident we are in it, so you can see the reasoning rather than take it on trust.

Because collection and analysis are automated, the brief follows your assessment quickly rather than after weeks of manual work.

Where AI-assisted methods are genuinely relevant to your business, the brief says so and explains what changes in practice. Where they are not, it says that too, rather than repeating whatever is currently in the news.

A brief is a judgement about likelihood and priority. It is not a prediction, and it does not replace the fixes themselves, which are quoted separately.

Common questions

Do we need an assessment first?

Yes. The brief is built on your own exposure and vulnerability findings. Without them it would be general advice that could be written for any business.

Is this a generic industry report?

No. Every conclusion ties back to something found in your public information or your agreed systems, and the brief names the finding it came from.

Who is it written for?

The person deciding what to spend. It is meant to be read by an owner, manager or board without translation, with the technical detail kept aside for your IT provider.

Does it predict attacks?

No. It ranks likelihood and priority based on what was found at one point in time. It cannot tell you whether or when something will happen.

Next step

Once you know which threats apply, the last step is making sure you would notice them: logging, alerts and a hunt through your existing records.
