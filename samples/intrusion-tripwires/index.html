Tripwire deployment and response handover

A worked medium-scope deployment using two existing Linux VMs, two zones and four decoy services, with tested delivery and a practical responder runbook. The small package covers one VM, one zone and two services.

Fictional worked example. Sensors, names, addresses, test events, response timings and decisions are invented. This is not evidence of a real deployment or incident.

Additional technical example: this is a decoy deployment and alert handover. It demonstrates one detection technique, not a completed threat hunt, a comprehensive incident response plan or staffed monitoring. Any such deployment requires a separately agreed scope.

01 · Management decision

Executive summary

Four decoy services have been deployed across the office and server networks. Each generated a test alert that reached the nominated responder by email and SMS. Tests also confirmed sensor-loss notifications and automated escalation to the backup contact.

The decoys provide an additional opportunity to notice someone exploring internal systems. An interaction gives the responder a specific lead to investigate, although legitimate administrative tools can also trigger an alert. The decoys do not block access, and activity that does not touch them remains outside their coverage.

The main outstanding risk is response. Alerts may remain unattended outside the agreed business-hours window. Both delivery channels also depend on the same alert service, and routine activity needs continued review so meaningful alerts are not dismissed.

Recommended decisions

  • Confirm the primary and backup responders, their responsibilities and the coverage the business requires.
  • Agree how alerts outside that coverage will be handled and record any accepted response gap.
  • Assign the IT provider to review routine alerts, maintain sensor health and test what happens if the shared alert service fails.

The tested deployment is working within its agreed scope. Its ongoing value depends on maintained alert delivery and a person able to investigate; successful installation does not establish that the wider business is free from compromise.

How to read the references

Reference codes connect the report's records so you can trace a conclusion back to its source. The numbers distinguish individual records; priority, confidence and completion status are stated separately.

  • Sensor TW-OFFICE-01 / TW-SERVER-01: The two systems hosting the decoys, on the office and server networks respectively.
  • Decoy D-01: An individual service intended to reveal unexpected interaction. D-01 and D-02 are on the office sensor; D-03 and D-04 are on the server sensor.
  • Validation test TT-01: A recorded check of a decoy trigger, sensor-health notification or backup escalation.
  • Evidence T-E01: The supporting capture, configuration or review record. The evidence index describes each item.
  • Follow-up action TW-A01: An unresolved operating decision or improvement. TW-A01 covers routine alerts, TW-A02 response coverage and TW-A03 the shared alert-service failure path.

The report reference in the document details identifies the report as a whole. Client evidence references in these public examples are illustrative; no private evidence files are attached.

02 · Design intent

Scope, placement and boundaries

The agreed objective is to make unexpected internal discovery visible to a named person. One sensor sits on the office network and one on the server network, with services selected to resemble resources an unauthorised user might investigate. The deployment uses one existing shared alert receiver, with its existing email/SMS routes, and the existing health-monitoring facility. It includes two scheduled tuning reviews and handover. New platforms, physical installation and file-share integration are excluded.

The office sensor can be reached from the agreed staff test segment; the server sensor from the agreed server test segment. The test does not establish visibility from every VLAN or remote-access route. Guest wireless, branch networks, cloud-only activity and personal devices were excluded from this deployment.

Consequence of limited placement: An intruder using a cloud account or moving along another network route may never touch a decoy. No alert in that case says nothing about the intrusion. Keep the wider identity, endpoint and network investigation requirements in the response plan; adding decoys should not lead the business to retire those sources.

Decoys contain only invented content and have no production credentials. They do not grant access to business file shares or use privileged domain accounts. Sensor administration is restricted to the management route; approved outbound connections support health and alert delivery. The configuration record identifies permitted paths and dependencies. SSH and web modules emulate service interactions; no Samba file share is installed. Synthetic credential-like fields are redacted before local storage and forwarding; T-E09 records the test against both sinks.

Placement was reviewed with the IT provider to avoid conflicting names and addresses. No business workflow should depend on the decoys. Their presence does not replace endpoint protection, logging, access controls or investigation of a suspected incident.

Availability and backup escalation

TT-05 stopped the office sensor heartbeat at 11:00. The availability email arrived at 11:16 and SMS at 11:17; the primary acknowledged at 11:20. The sensor returned at 11:25 and a new decoy trigger confirmed recovery at 11:27. T-E05 holds the event sequence.

TT-06 repeated the loss test on the server sensor at 13:00. Email arrived at 13:16, SMS at 13:17 and acknowledgement at 13:21. The sensor returned at 13:25; its recovery trigger passed at 13:27. T-E06 records the result. These tests exercise loss of a sensor, not every possible failure of the central alert service.

TT-07 deliberately left a 14:00 test alert unacknowledged. The backup received escalation email at 14:15:20 and SMS at 14:15:34, then acknowledged at 14:18. T-E07 records that exercise. Both channels depend on parts of the same alert platform; they are not fully independent protection against a platform outage.

05 · What normal looked like

Baseline activity and tuning decisions

During 1–14 September, excluding acceptance tests, three interactions came from the provider’s scheduled inventory scanner and one from an administrator’s network check. All four were reconciled to approved activity using scan schedules and change notes in T-E08. Four events across ten business days are not enough to infer a reliable monthly rate.

The scanner source is labelled “known inventory source” for context, but its alerts remain visible. No blanket source exclusions were applied. A familiar address can change ownership, and the same system could later perform unexpected activity. Responders verify the timing and task before dismissing an event.

If volume becomes disruptive, review the actual source, schedule and service before changing the rule. Any suppression must record its scope, approver, reason, expiry and what detection it removes. Repeat the affected trigger and escalation tests after a change. Activity summaries should distinguish tests, verified routine activity and unexplained interactions.

Tuning record: reviews on 7 and 14 September reconciled the four ordinary-activity clusters and confirmed the labels and no-suppression decision. Ten business days of observation were completed within the medium package’s cap of 20 initial alert clusters. T-E08 records both reviews.

Unresolved limitation: only ten business days of ordinary activity were observed. The IT provider should review a representative period of activity with the business owner before proposing further tuning. No claim of “zero false positives” or guaranteed detection rate is made.

06 · Acting on an alert

Responder runbook

Primary role: client IT provider service desk. Backup role: client operations manager. Decision authority: business owner for disruptive containment unless the incident plan delegates that authority. A live handover replaces these fictional roles with named people, tested contact details and agreed authority.

The sample service window is 08:30–17:00 AEST on business days. The 15-minute backup notification is an automated escalation setting, not a guaranteed human response. Alerts still send outside those hours, but no staffed overnight or weekend response is promised. Both recipients being unavailable remains a material gap.

  1. Acknowledge and preserve: record the event ID, source address, sensor, service and time in the incident ticket. Preserve the original alert and relevant logs. An availability alert starts a collection-health check; an interaction alert starts the triage below.
  2. Identify the source: use time-matched network and asset records to locate the device and owner. Consider address reassignment, shared systems and approved scanners. Do not assume that the current holder of an address generated an earlier event.
  3. Check authorised activity: compare the timestamp with approved maintenance and scanning. Confirm through an established provider contact. If explained, record the matching change or scan reference and close as verified routine activity.
  4. Escalate unexplained interaction: contact the primary incident lead and backup, record whether the device touched more than one decoy, and preserve related endpoint, identity and firewall records. Additional suspicious evidence increases urgency; the tripwire alone does not prove compromise.
  5. Contain under authority: use the incident plan and authorised isolation procedure if containment is warranted. Record who approved it and what business function may be interrupted. Avoid deleting files, wiping or rebooting the source device before evidence and operational needs are considered.
  6. Record the outcome: retain the rationale, evidence references and follow-up owner. If an incident is confirmed or remains plausible, continue under the incident response process. Acknowledging the notification is not equivalent to resolving the event.

If neither responder is reachable, the configured route has exhausted its intended recipients. The business owner must decide whether to buy or arrange additional response coverage; adding another delivery channel alone does not provide that staffing.

Follow-up action TW-A01 · Routine alerts can hide a meaningful interaction

Observed and consequence: Four ordinary-activity clusters were explained during ten business days. That is too little history to establish a reliable monthly volume. If routine alerts become repetitive and nobody reviews their causes, responders may ignore a later interaction that needs investigation; broad suppression could hide it altogether.

Decision: Keep the alerts visible, reconcile events to approved work, and review a representative activity record with the business owner. Any tuning needs a recorded reason, scope and expiry plus a repeat trigger test. A familiar source address is context, not proof that every future event is benign.

Follow-up action TW-A02 · An overnight alert may wait until staff return

Observed and consequence: Alerts send outside business hours, but no staffed overnight or weekend response is agreed. An intruder interacting with a decoy could therefore generate a correctly delivered alert while continuing to explore systems before anyone acts. The 15-minute automated escalation setting does not shorten that delay if both contacts are unavailable.

Decision: The business owner must arrange a reachable responder for the required hours or explicitly accept the delay and its possible consequences. Record that choice and revisit it when business needs or responder availability change. Until coverage changes, state the limitation in the incident plan; an additional messaging channel alone does not supply a responder.

Follow-up action TW-A03 · Both notification channels share a failure point

Observed and consequence: Email and SMS depend on the same central alert service. The completed tests prove delivery during those tests and sensor-loss detection; they do not prove an independent way to detect or recover from a central-service outage. During such an outage, decoy interactions could produce no usable notification through either route.

Decision: The IT provider should test the central delivery-outage procedure in an approved change window, including how the outage is noticed, the alternate contact route and how affected events are recovered or recorded as lost. Without this, a quiet notification stream could be incorrectly treated as an absence of suspicious activity.

Patch sensors in an agreed change window, retain a configuration backup and repeat the affected trigger tests afterwards. Recheck placement when networks are segmented or services move. Decommission unused sensors through the asset register and remove obsolete alert routes so stale records do not appear healthy.

Operating arrangements: Agree who checks sensor health, reviews routine events, runs controlled tests and investigates unexplained interactions. Platform licences, message charges and response support need their own confirmed commercial terms. Ongoing monitoring and response require an agreed service scope.

08 · Delivery and residual risk

Acceptance record and evidence index

The proposed acceptance record approves the two sensors and four services for the tested segments, with the after-hours, short-baseline and central-platform limitations explicitly recorded. The walkthrough asks the primary and backup to locate an event, acknowledge it and describe the first triage steps. Open actions TW-A01–03 remain on the handover tracker.

Evidence index: T-E01–04 hold decoy tests and recipient confirmation; T-E05–06 hold sensor-loss and recovery tests; T-E07 holds backup escalation; T-E08 holds the ordinary-activity review; T-E09 holds inventory, configuration and management-access records; T-E10 holds the walkthrough and exception checklist. These are illustrative references, with no real capture files attached.

A real delivery supplies the controlled evidence pack, exact sensor inventory, alert routing diagram, contact and authority register, response runbook, maintenance schedule and signed acceptance or exception record. Client-sensitive addresses and actual contact details are kept out of public copies. Version 1.0 is the initial example issue.

A successful test proves that one planned interaction was detected and delivered under the tested conditions. It cannot show that an attacker will touch a decoy, that every network route is covered or that no previous incident occurred. Investigating an existing concern requires a separate threat-hunting or incident investigation scope.
